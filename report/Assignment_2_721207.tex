\section{Introduction}

ZOZOTOWN, a large Japanese fashion website, shows every visitor a row of three
recommended items. Which items should go into that row, and in which slot, to
get the most clicks? With dozens of candidate items and click rates well below
one percent, the site cannot afford to test every item at length: every
impression spent on a weak item is a click lost. This is a multi-armed bandit
problem. Each item is an arm, a click is the reward, and the policy has to
balance trying uncertain items (exploration) against showing the items that
look best so far (exploitation).

We use a week of logged data from a randomised experiment that ZOZO ran in
November 2019 \citep{saito2021} and ask which items to show, in which slot,
and which bandit policy finds them fastest. The two algorithms are Thompson
Sampling (TS) and the Upper Confidence Bound algorithm (UCB), both evaluated
offline with the replay method of \citet{li2011}. We extend them in four
ways: TS with an informed prior and UCB with a bonus scaled to the click
rates (question c), one model per user group that borrows from the pooled
model (d), a minimum exposure guarantee for items (e), and discounted TS and
sliding window UCB for non-stationary data (g).

The main findings are these. On the 80 item file, UCB with a bonus scaled to
the size of the click rates raises the CTR from 0.330\% under random serving
to 0.502\%, and TS with a prior near the real click rate reaches 0.413\%. With
the textbook settings both stay close to random. The gains point the same way
in most resamples of the week, but one week of data is too little to call
any of them significant once the dependence between rows is taken into
account. The slot does not change which items are best. Learning in large
batches hurts UCB more than TS. Personalising by user group pays off only for
the feature with a few large groups, and only when the group models borrow
from the pooled model. UCB earns its clicks by showing a handful of items and
starving the rest. A floor that guarantees every item half its fair share of
exposure ends this at a cost of about 0.13 percentage points of CTR. Finally,
the daily click level shifts during the week but the item ranking does not.
Forgetting old data therefore does not help on this log, although it does
when we make the ranking change.

\section{Data}

The main file has 1{,}356{,}670 impressions of 79 items over seven days
(24 to 30 November 2019), with 4{,}720 clicks, an overall click-through rate
(CTR) of 0.348\%. Each row records one item shown in one slot (1 = left,
2 = centre, 3 = right), whether it was clicked, its propensity score and four
hashed user features. Two smaller files contain the same log restricted to 10
items (172{,}400 rows, CTR 0.280\%) and 20 items (345{,}469 rows, CTR 0.255\%);
the 10 item file has no user features. We use the 80 item file for all main
results and the 10 item file as an easier setting with fewer arms
(code Table~1). The 80 item file has 79 items because one of the 80 items
that the logging policy drew from never appears in the log (the item ids run
from 1 to 79).

Three facts about the data shape the rest of the analysis. First, the logging
policy was uniform random. Every row has propensity $0.0125 = 1/80$ (code
Table~2). The brief mentions that ZOZO also ran a Bernoulli TS policy, but
those impressions are not in this file, so uniform random serving is both the
policy that generated our data and our benchmark. Uniform logging is also what
makes unbiased replay evaluation possible (Section~\ref{sec:replay}).

Second, items differ but positions hardly do. Item CTRs range from 0.115\% to
0.784\% (Figure~\ref{fig:itemctr}), so the best item is clicked almost seven
times as often as the worst. A bandit can exploit that spread. The three
positions have almost the same CTR: 0.354\%, 0.349\% and 0.341\% (code
Table~3).

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem setup}

There are $K$ items (arms). When item $k$ is shown it is clicked with an
unknown probability $\theta_k$. ZOZO assumes that the click depends only on
the item and its position \citep{saito2021}; Section~\ref{sec:a} shows that
the position has no detectable effect on which items are best, so the main
policies use one $\theta_k$ per item. At every impression $t$ a policy
chooses an item $\pi_t$ and aims to maximise the expected number of clicks, or
equivalently to minimise the regret
%
\begin{equation}
  R_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \big(\theta^{*} - \theta_{\pi_t}\big),
  \qquad \theta^{*} = \max_k \theta_k ,
  \label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
%
the number of clicks lost against always showing the best item. Exploring
an uncertain item costs regret now but can reveal a better item for later;
the policies below differ in how they make that trade.

\subsection{Offline evaluation by replay}
\label{sec:replay}

We cannot show items to real visitors, so every policy is evaluated on the
log with the replay method \citep{li2011}. Let the log be a sequence of
events $(x_t, a_t, r_t)$, $t = 1,\dots,T$, in time order, where $x_t$ is the
context (slot and user features), $a_t$ the item that was shown and
$r_t \in \{0,1\}$ the click. A policy $\pi$ walks through the log. At each
event it picks an item $\pi(x_t, h_{t-1})$ based on its history $h_{t-1}$. If
the pick equals the logged item, the event is \emph{matched}: the click is
revealed, counted, and added to the history. Otherwise the event is skipped
and the policy learns nothing. The replay estimate of the policy's CTR is
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\text{CTR}}(\pi)
  = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t \,\mathbf{1}\{\pi(x_t,h_{t-1}) = a_t\}}
         {\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{1}\{\pi(x_t,h_{t-1}) = a_t\}} .
  \label{eq:replay}
\end{equation}
%
\citet{li2011} show that this estimate is unbiased when the logged item was
chosen uniformly at random and independently of the click, and the events are
independent. The first condition holds by design. With $K$ arms, about one
event in $K$ matches, so both the 10 and the 80 item file leave roughly
17{,}000 matched events and only 45 to 130 clicks per run. One standard error
of a CTR estimate is 0.04 to 0.07 percentage points, and we treat differences
of that size as noise.

The independence condition holds only approximately. In the log, the same
item often stays in the same slot for many consecutive rows. A deterministic
policy such as UCB keeps its pick until it learns something new, so it
matches one row of such a streak and then waits for the next streak of its
new favourite. On the 10 item file, UCB with $c=1$ matches 4{,}977 events in
time order but 17{,}227 when the rows are shuffled (code Table~18). We report
matched events next to every CTR and come back to this in
Section~\ref{sec:c}.

Each stochastic policy is replayed 10 times in the main comparison (seeds 1
to 10, code Table~13) and 5 times in questions (b) to (g) (seeds 1 to 5); run
$i$ uses random seed $1+i$. The same policy can therefore show different
levels in different sections. UCB with $c=0.01$ scores 0.502\% over 10 runs
and 0.569\% over the first 5, but its CTR moves by about 0.11 percentage
points from run to run (code Table~16), so the two agree within noise. Tables
report the mean CTR over runs. The code tables also give the spread across
runs and the binomial standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$ of one run
with $n$ matched events, and we quote these in the text where a comparison
depends on them. The benchmark at the top is the \emph{Oracle}, which always
shows the item with the highest CTR over the whole week. No real policy has
that information, so the Oracle is an upper bound for any policy that shows
the same item to everyone.

\subsection{Thompson Sampling}

TS keeps a Beta belief about the click probability $\theta_k$ of every item
$k$. Starting from the prior $\text{Beta}(a_0, b_0)$, after $s_k$ clicks and
$f_k$ non-clicks the posterior is
%
\begin{equation}
  \theta_k \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Beta}(a_0 + s_k,\; b_0 + f_k),
  \label{eq:ts}
\end{equation}
%
by Beta-Bernoulli conjugacy. At every event TS draws one value
$\tilde\theta_k$ from each posterior and shows $\arg\max_k \tilde\theta_k$;
after a matched event it adds one to $s_k$ or $f_k$ \citep{russo2018}.
Items with an uncertain posterior sometimes draw a high value and get tried,
so exploration comes from the posterior itself. The prior can be read as
$a_0-1$ imagined clicks and $b_0-1$ imagined non-clicks with mean
$a_0/(a_0+b_0)$. The default $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ is flat over $[0,1]$.

\subsection{Upper Confidence Bound}

UCB1 \citep{auer2002} first shows every item once and then shows the item with
the highest index
%
\begin{equation}
  U_k = \hat\mu_k + c\,\sqrt{\frac{2\ln n}{n_k}},
  \label{eq:ucb}
\end{equation}
%
where $\hat\mu_k$ is the item's CTR so far, $n_k$ the number of times it was
shown and $n$ the total number of matched events. The second term is an
optimism bonus that shrinks as an item is shown more. The textbook version has
$c=1$; we treat $c$ as a tuning parameter. Unlike TS, UCB is deterministic
given its history: the same history always gives the same choice. Its runs
still differ, because in the first round every item is tried once in random
order and ties between equal indices are broken at random.

\subsection{Measures}

Besides the CTR we report the lift over Random,
$100(\widehat{\text{CTR}}/\widehat{\text{CTR}}_{\text{Random}} - 1)$, the
regret per 10{,}000 matched events,
$10^4(\widehat{\text{CTR}}_{\text{Oracle}} - \widehat{\text{CTR}})$, which
estimates the regret~\eqref{eq:regret} per 10{,}000 impressions with the
Oracle CTR in place of $\theta^*$.

To judge whether a lift is real we use an hour-block bootstrap instead of a
textbook test. A two-proportion $z$-test against Random assumes independent
rows, and Section~\ref{sec:f} shows that rows within the same hour are not
independent. One bootstrap resample draws the 168 hours of the week with
replacement and puts them one after the other in the drawn order, so the
streaks inside each hour stay intact. Random, TS with $\text{Beta}(1,300)$,
UCB with $c=0.01$ and per group TS are each replayed once on the resample
(seed $1+b$ for resample $b$), and we record each policy's lift over the
Random run on the same resample. With 100 resamples, the 2.5th and 97.5th
percentiles of these lifts give a 95\% interval. The two kinds of spread
answer different questions. The spread across runs measures only the
policy's own randomness on a fixed log. The bootstrap also lets the data
vary, as another week with the same hourly dependence might, so its
intervals are wider. Because Random is itself one noisy run in each
resample, the intervals are on the conservative side.

The two files behave differently. With 10 items every learning policy except

\subsection{Where to place the items}

The brief also asks where to place the items. If the best item depended on
the slot, a policy should learn a separate ranking for the left, centre and
right position. The five best items per position overlap heavily (items 33,
34, 38, 57 and 59 appear in several slots, code Table~10). A likelihood ratio
test of the logistic model with item and position effects against a model
with a separate rate for every item and position rejects at the textbook
level ($\text{LR}=332.0$, 156 degrees of freedom, $p<10^{-14}$). That test,
however, assumes independent rows. When we swap the three position labels
between slots at random within every hour, which keeps the streaks intact but
removes any real link between slot and item, the placebo statistics average
323.3 and the permutation $p$-value is 0.38 (code Table~11). We find no
evidence that the best item depends on the slot. In line with this, TS with
one model per position does not beat one model for all positions: per position
from zero gives 0.399\%, per position with borrowing (Section~\ref{sec:d})
0.395\%, against 0.387\% for one model, all within one standard error of
about 0.05 (code Table~12). The practical answer is to show the
best items and not to spend data on learning a ranking per slot.

\section{Question (b): batch size}
\label{sec:b}

A production system does not update after every impression; it collects
results and updates in batches. With batch size $B$ the policy keeps its
beliefs fixed for $B$ matched events and then processes all of them at once.
We vary $B$ from 1 to 5{,}000 for TS and for UCB with $c=0.01$
(Table~\ref{tab:b}).

On the 10 item file both policies lose clicks as batches grow, but in
different ways. TS loses some of its advantage and then levels off; at
$B=5{,}000$, which leaves only three or four updates in the whole week, it
still gets 0.353\%, well above Random. UCB hardly suffers up to $B=100$ and
then falls to 0.274\%, Random level. The reason lies in the algorithms.
During a batch UCB's index does not change, so it shows the same item for
the whole batch and repeats a weak choice thousands of times. TS keeps drawing
from its posterior and spreads the batch over several promising items. This
agrees with \citet{chapelle2011}, who found that TS copes well with delayed
feedback. On the 80 item file the picture is weaker. TS barely learns at any
batch size, so it has little to lose, and UCB varies so much between runs
(standard deviation up to 0.13 percentage points) that the drop is not clear
beyond $B=1$. The matched counts show a second effect. While UCB holds one
choice during a batch it also catches the streaks in the log, and its matched
events rise from 12{,}930 at $B=1$ to about 17{,}000 at $B\geq 100$
(10 items).

\section{Question (c): tuning}
\label{sec:c}

We vary the prior of TS and the bonus scale $c$ of UCB (Figure~\ref{fig:tuning}).

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \codefigure{figure_5_tuning_80items.png}{0.9}
  \caption{CTR for TS with prior $\text{Beta}(1,b)$ (left) and UCB with bonus
    scale $c$ (right) on the 80 item file, 5 runs per setting, with
    $\pm 1.96$ binomial standard errors; the dashed line is Random
    (code Figure~5, code Tables~15 and 16).}
  \label{fig:tuning}
\end{figure}

For TS the prior matters most when there are many arms. On the 80 item file
CTR rises from 0.387\% with the flat prior to 0.420\% with $\text{Beta}(1,300)$
and 0.483\% with $\text{Beta}(1,1000)$, whose prior means of 0.33\% and 0.10\%
are on the scale of the real CTRs. A flat prior claims that a CTR of 50\% is
as likely as one of 0.3\%, and the data spends most of its clicks correcting
that claim. The most pessimistic prior does best because clicks are scarce.
With a prior mean of 0.10\% an item has to earn its exposure: an item without
clicks loses out after a few dozen shows, and the matched events go to the
items that do get clicked. On the 10 item file,
with about 1{,}700 matched events per item, the data overrides any prior and
all settings lie within noise of each other (0.31 to 0.42\%, code Table~15).

UCB is more sensitive. On the 10 item file CTR follows a hill in $c$: 0.300\%
at $c=1$, 0.444\% at $c=0.03$ and 0.341\% at $c=0.001$. A large bonus keeps
UCB exploring; a very small one makes it commit to whichever item looked best
early, which is sometimes an item that was lucky. On the 80 item file the best
values are $c=0.01$ (0.569\%) and $c=0.1$ (0.528\%) (code Table~16). UCB1
was designed for rewards on the scale of $[0,1]$; here it only works once the
bonus is shrunk to the scale of the CTRs.

These best settings were chosen on the same log on which they are scored, so
they flatter the tuned policies. As a fairer check we choose the setting on
days 1 and 2 only and score it on days 3 to 7 (code Table~17). On the 80 item
file the choice for UCB is $c=0.1$, which scores 0.502\% on the later days
against 0.424\% for the default and 0.378\% for Random, so tuning UCB helps out
of sample. The choice for TS, $\text{Beta}(1,30)$, does not help (0.372\%
against 0.381\%). On the 10 item file the first two days leave only about a dozen
matched clicks, too few to separate the settings, and the defaults are chosen. In
practice $c$ and the prior have to be set before launch, ideally from earlier
data on the site's typical CTR.

Finally, replaying the log in shuffled order removes the streaks. UCB with
$c=1$ then matches 17{,}112 events instead of 10{,}639 on the 80 item file
(code Table~18). The CTRs of the learning policies are lower in shuffled
order in all six policy and file combinations, in five of them by 0.05 to
0.11 percentage points (for example 0.569\% against 0.501\% for UCB with
$c=0.01$); the sixth, UCB with $c=1$ on 10 items, hardly changes. Random does
not show this gap: it scores 0.341\% in time order and 0.355\% shuffled on the
80 item file, and 0.274\% against 0.279\% on the 10 item file. Each gap is
only about one standard error. Still, it appears only for policies that adapt
and always in the same direction, which suggests that replay on this log
slightly favours policies that stay with one item, perhaps because clicks
cluster within streaks. The ranking of the policies does not change, but
absolute CTRs should be read with this caveat.

\section{Question (d): one model or one per user group}
\label{sec:d}

So far every visitor gets the same recommendation, and Section~\ref{sec:a}
found no reason to change that across positions. User groups are a different
matter. If they prefer different items, one model per group could do better,
but each group model then learns from only a fraction of the data. We compare three designs for each of
the four user features: one pooled TS, one TS per level of the feature
starting from $\text{Beta}(1,1)$, and one TS per level that borrows from the
pooled model. In the last design the prior of item $k$, in every group, is
%
\begin{equation}
  a_{0,k} = 1 + w_k\,\bar p_k, \qquad b_{0,k} = 1 + w_k\,(1-\bar p_k),
  \qquad w_k = \min(m, N_k),
  \label{eq:borrow}
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar p_k$ and $N_k$ are the CTR and the number of matched events of
item $k$ in the pooled model so far and $m=1{,}000$. A small group stays close
to the pooled estimate; a large group soon outweighs the borrowed $m$ shows
with its own data. This is shrinkage towards the pooled mean. The pooled
counts are built during the replay from past events only, so the policy uses
no information that a live system would not have. A group's own clicks are
counted in both terms, which double counts them slightly; the cap $m$ keeps
this small.

percentage points has a standard error of about 0.07, so $z\approx1.5$, and
it is the best of four features, which makes a gain of this size more likely
by chance. We read it as suggestive, not as proven. For the features with
ten levels, many of them small, borrowing makes no difference.

Table~20). Group 3, with 150{,}691 rows, is the clearest case: the
pooled model lifts its CTR by 14\% over Random, the per group model with
borrowing by 100\%, so this group prefers different items from the majority.
Group 0 has 6{,}641 rows and about 80 matched events per run, too few to
learn anything or even to measure how well it is served. One pooled model
ignores the differences between groups, and fully separate models waste data.
Borrowing sits in between and pays off only where groups are large enough.

We do not fit a contextual bandit such as LinUCB or logistic TS. The user
features are hashed category codes with no order or meaning, so a linear
model could only use them as one-hot group dummies, with item by group
interactions to let preferences differ. That is a parametric version of the
per group models with shrinkage above, and with about 60 to 85 clicks per run
(code Table~19) it has no extra information to learn from.

\section{Question (e): fairness}
\label{sec:e}

A policy that maximises clicks can be unfair in two ways. It can concentrate
exposure on a few items, so that the other sellers are hardly seen and an item
that was unlucky early never gets a second chance (fairness to items). It can
also serve some user groups much better than others, usually small groups or
groups whose taste differs from the majority (fairness to users).

We measure fairness to items with the Gini coefficient of the exposure shares
$e_k$ (the share of matched events in which item $k$ was shown),
%
\begin{equation}
  G = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{K} i\, e_{(i)}}{K\sum_{i} e_{(i)}} - \frac{K+1}{K},
  \label{eq:gini}
\end{equation}
%
with $e_{(1)}\leq\dots\leq e_{(K)}$, so $G=0$ means equal exposure. We also
count the items that receive less than half of the fair share $1/K$, and
report the rank correlation between exposure and item CTR, which shows
whether exposure follows merit. For fairness to users we compute, per level
of a user feature, the CTR divided by Random's CTR in the same
group (the lift), and report the worst group's lift and the gap between the
best and the worst group. The lift removes differences in how often each
group clicks anyway (under random serving the four groups click at 0.18\%
to 0.38\%, code Table~5), so a policy is not counted as unfair for serving a
group that rarely clicks. Groups with fewer than 500 matched events per run
are left out because their lift cannot be estimated; on the 80 item file this
excludes group 0.

As the fairness extension we add a minimum exposure guarantee. A wrapper around
any policy promises every item at least a fraction $\phi$ of its fair share:
whenever some item has been shown fewer than $\phi\, n/K$ times after $n$
matched events, the wrapper shows the most neglected item; otherwise the base
policy decides. We put the floor on UCB, the policy that concentrates
exposure, with $\phi = 0.25$ and $\phi = 0.5$. The floor is the extension on
the item side; per group TS with borrowing from question (d) is the
extension on the user side, and we score both on the same measures.

because its few favourite items suit the majority. TS is close to fair on
both counts: its random draws spread exposure ($G=0.09$), exposure follows
merit (rank correlation 0.69), and every group gains 10 to 15\%. The price is
a lower CTR. On \texttt{user\_feature\_0}, per group TS with borrowing gives
users the best deal: the highest worst-group lift (1.28) with a moderate Gini
of 0.16, because it serves each large group with its own favourites.

The floor does what it promises. The least shown item gets 0.32\% of the
exposure with $\phi=0.25$ and 0.63\% with $\phi=0.5$, exactly a quarter and a
half of the fair share of 1.27\% (code Table~21). The weak floor changes
little: the CTR stays at 0.57\%, the Gini falls only from 0.67 to 0.63, and 50
items are still below half their share, which this floor does not rule out.
It even widens the gap between user groups, as group 3 drops to a lift of 0.81
(code Table~22). The floor at half the fair share changes much more. No item
is starved, the share of the top three items falls from 41\% to 17\%, the
Gini halves to 0.35, and exposure follows merit more closely (rank
correlation 0.54 against 0.24). Every group now gains on Random (lifts 1.08
to 1.33). The price is about 0.13 percentage points of CTR, from 0.57\% to
0.44\%, which still leaves the policy above TS and Random. Per group TS with
borrowing, however, scores better on CTR, Gini and worst group lift (0.49\%,
0.16 and 1.28), although its gap between groups is wider. For ZOZO the floor
is a simple guarantee that can be added to any policy. Personalisation is the
cheaper way to be fair to both sides, but only for \texttt{user\_feature\_0},

\section{Question (f): is the data stationary?}
\label{sec:f}

Two kinds of change matter differently for a bandit. If all items become more
or less popular together, the best item stays the best and a stationary policy
still picks correctly. If the ranking of the items changes, a policy that
averages over the whole past keeps showing yesterday's winner. We test both on
the 80 item file.

All textbook tests assume independent rows, and the streaks in the log violate
that. We therefore calibrate every test with a block permutation placebo: the
168 hours of the week are dealt out at random into placebo days (or halves),
keeping each hour intact, and the test statistic is recomputed 200 times. A
placebo split keeps the dependence between nearby rows but contains no real
trend, so it shows how large the statistic gets by chance. The permutation
$p$-value is the share of placebo statistics at least as extreme as the real
one.

The textbook tests all reject stationarity, but the placebo values show why
they cannot be trusted here: even random groupings of hours give a $\chi^2$ of
12.4 on average instead of the 6 expected under independence
(Table~\ref{tab:f}). After calibration the results split. The overall daily
CTR does change: the real $\chi^2$ of 27.5 beats 98.5\% of the placebo values
(permutation $p=0.015$), with Wednesday at 0.303\% and Tuesday at 0.382\%. The
item ranking does not. The rank correlation of item CTRs between days 1 to 3
and days 4 to 7 is 0.772, a typical value for a placebo split of hours (mean
0.747, $p=0.74$; Figure~\ref{fig:halves}). The likelihood ratio tests of an
item by period interaction in a logistic model of the click give statistics
below the placebo mean ($p=0.89$ by half week and $p=0.82$ by day). In the
day-level test 23\% of the cells have fewer than five expected clicks, which
is one more reason to rely on the permutation version.

level shift does not change which item is best, so it does not hurt a bandit
that only needs the ranking.

\section{Question (g): policies for a changing environment}
\label{sec:g}

If the ranking did change, the policies would have to stop weighting old and
new evidence equally. We implement two such changes and outline a third.

Discounted TS \citep{raj2017} multiplies all counts by $\gamma<1$ after every
matched event before adding the new result,
%
\begin{equation}
  s_k \leftarrow \gamma s_k + r\,\mathbf{1}\{k=a\}, \qquad
  f_k \leftarrow \gamma f_k + (1-r)\,\mathbf{1}\{k=a\},
  \label{eq:dts}
\end{equation}
%
and samples from $\text{Beta}(a_0+s_k, b_0+f_k)$. An event that is $n$ updates
old keeps weight $\gamma^n$, a memory of about $1/(1-\gamma)$ updates; we use
$\gamma \in \{0.9999, 0.999, 0.995\}$. Sliding window UCB
\citep{garivier2011} computes $\hat\mu_k$ and $n_k$ in~\eqref{eq:ucb} from the
last $W$ matched events only, with $W \in \{5{,}000, 2{,}000, 1{,}000\}$ and
$c=0.01$. The third option is change point detection, which monitors each
item's recent CTR and restarts the policy when it departs from the long-run
value. With 45 to 130 clicks per run spread over 10 to 79 items, a detector
would have far too little data per item to tell a real change from noise, so
we do not implement it.

Because the ranking in our data is stable (Section~\ref{sec:f}), we also build
a log in which it is not: from the middle of the week the item labels are
reversed by rank, so the rows of the worst item now carry the label of the
best item, the second worst that of the second best, and so on. The logging
stays uniform, so replay remains valid. The cost of the flip is a policy's CTR
in the second half of the real log minus its CTR in the second half of the
flipped log.

difference, and the shortest window costs UCB 0.06. Throwing away old clicks does not pay when
the ranking is stable and clicks are scarce. On the flipped log the forgetting
policies adapt better. The flip costs plain TS 0.19 percentage points on the
10 item file and every discounted version at most 0.04. On the same file
sliding window UCB pays between nothing and half of the 0.15 that plain UCB
pays. Figure~\ref{fig:shift} shows the mechanism: plain TS drops to about
0.1\% after the flip and needs around 15{,}000 rows to recover, while the
forgetting policies dip less and recover sooner. On the 80 item file the
effects are smaller, since TS had learned little before the flip and has
little to unlearn. Discounted TS with $\gamma=0.9999$ is the only version
with a noticeable gain there, and the longest window does not help UCB at
all (cost 0.106 against 0.103).

The choice of model therefore depends on the type of change. On the ZOZO
data, with a level shift and a stable ranking, plain TS or UCB is the better
choice. Forgetting is the right tool only when evidence suggests that the
ranking moves, and its memory length should then be set as long as the speed
of change allows.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}

Three caveats qualify these results. First, replay leaves about 17{,}000
matched events and fewer than 130 clicks per run, so most differences between
policies are within one or two standard errors. Once the dependence between
rows is respected, not even the 52\% lift of UCB is significant: the
hour-block bootstrap puts it between $-17\%$ and $150\%$. The data support
the direction, with the tuned policies ahead of Random in 73 to 94\% of
resamples, but not the size of the gain. Second, replay assumes independent
events, and the streaks in the log break that assumption. They cut the number
of matched events for deterministic policies, and the shuffled replay
suggests that they also raise the CTR of policies that stay with one item by
up to about 0.1 percentage points (Section~\ref{sec:c}). This does not change
which policies come out ahead, but it makes absolute levels less certain.
Third, the tuned settings were chosen on the same week of data. The out of
sample check supports tuning UCB but not tuning TS. Beyond these, the data cover a single week that includes Black
Friday (29 November), so the stationarity results may not carry over to other
periods. The three slots are evaluated one at a time, because the log does
not record which three items were shown together. And the user features are
hashed, so we can say that small groups are served worse, but not who they
are.

For ZOZOTOWN our advice is to show the items with the highest click rate, in
any slot, since the slot does not change which items are best. Learn them with a bandit whose exploration is scaled
to how rare clicks are. We recommend UCB with $c$ between 0.01 and 0.1. With
$c=0.01$ it lifts the CTR by 52\% over random serving on the 80 item file,
but that setting was chosen on the same week, so the lift is optimistic, and
its bootstrap interval is wide. The
fairer check supports the range: $c=0.1$, chosen on days 1 and 2 only,
scores 0.502\% on days 3 to 7 against 0.424\% for the default $c=1$ and
0.378\% for Random (code Table~17). The textbook settings of both algorithms
stay close to random. TS is the safer choice when settings cannot be tuned in
advance or updates arrive in large batches, and it spreads exposure more
evenly over items and user groups. If UCB is used, an exposure floor of
half the fair share keeps every item visible, at a cost of about 0.13
percentage points of CTR. Group models that borrow from the pooled
model add clicks where groups are large. The item ranking was stable within
the week, so there is no need to forget old data, but a production system
should monitor the ranking and switch to a forgetting policy once it starts
to move.
